GMZ RESEARCH KNOWLEDGE BASE — EVIDENCE GAPS
Study 001, compiled 15 August 2026
========================================================================

These are questions the research could NOT answer from any tiered source.
Several are things the trade repeats as settled fact. They are recorded here
so nobody, human or agent, fills them in from memory or from a marketing page.

Where a gap matters to a live decision, the cheapest fix is usually GMZ's own
records rather than more searching. Ninety days of logged leads settles the
qualification question. One month of truck GPS settles drive time. The
callback history settles warranty.

========================================================================

  1. Whether a design only agreement written by a licensed California
     contractor is a home improvement contract under B&P 7151.2, and so
     subject to the deposit cap and mandated contents. Unresolved by
     statute text and by any regulator guidance found. Requires a
     California construction attorney, not more research.
  2. Whether B&P 7027.5's design authority reaches a design only engagement the
     client may take to another builder. The statute ties design to work
     the contractor performs and supervises. Sections 7027.1, 7027.3 and
     16 CCR 832.27 were not read and should be, then reviewed by counsel.
  3. Conflict between two verified California sources on when the three day
     cancellation right attaches. The licensing board conditions it on
     where the contract was negotiated; B&P 7159 states the notice as
     required contract content. Counsel should reconcile before any
     agreement form is standardised.
  4. Typical design fee as a percentage of construction cost for residential
     landscape design build. Every figure located (10 to 20 percent, 6 to
     15, 8 to 15) traces to cost aggregator or contractor marketing pages.
     Would require a trade survey with a disclosed sample, which does not
     appear to exist.
  5. Whether initial consultations are charged, and typical amounts. No tiered
     source discloses it. The circulating $100 to $300 range traces to
     homeowner cost aggregator pages.
  6. How common it is to credit a design fee toward construction. Genuinely
     unmeasured; no survey of any tier asks the question.
  7. How common phased design agreements with explicit go or no go gates are in
     residential work. Unmeasured. The nearest analogue is capital project
     front end planning research on projects over $1.5 billion, whose
     magnitudes do not transfer.
  8. The Project Definition Rating Index threshold of under 200 for adequate
     scope definition. Not present on the accessible CII page; the
     underlying report is paywalled. Requires buying RS213-1 or a CII
     implementation resource.
  9. Close rates by process type in landscaping. The circulating 63 versus 38
     percent same day estimate figures are unsourced and are not in the
     association report they are attributed to. The roughly 55 percent
     industry average traces to an outsourced finance firm's marketing
     page.
 10. The value of qualifying leads before a site visit. No study of any tier
     quantifies it for residential contracting. Requires the firm's own
     ninety day lead log.
 11. Whether publishing a minimum project size or a starting price changes the
     mix or quality of inbound leads for a residential contractor. Nothing
     at any tier. The only reliable route is to publish a minimum, tag
     leads before and after, and compare qualified lead rate over a fixed
     window.
 12. Whether published FAQ or process content affects contractor selection or
     inquiry to consultation conversion. Appears unstudied for home
     services. Every inference here from regulator guidance to site
     content is an inferential leap and should be labelled as one.
 13. Whether publishing a coarse budget band on a project page affects lead
     quality. Unevidenced. The healthcare price transparency literature is
     the nearest analogue and transfers poorly.
 14. Whether disclosing design fee as a percentage of construction helps or
     hurts, which is how this firm sells design. No source at any tier
     addresses disclosure practice or buyer response for that structure.
 15. How homeowners actually choose between competing residential bids. The
     peer reviewed contractor selection literature covers public and
     commercial procurement with formal weighted tender evaluation, a
     different buyer under different rules. One relevant ASCE paper
     returned HTTP 403 and was not read.
 16. No California or Peninsula specific consumer research at any tier on how
     homeowners select landscape contractors or what they want to see
     before making contact. The licensing board brochure is prescriptive
     guidance, not consumer research. This is the largest transferability
     problem in the base.
 17. No post 2015 tiered measurement of consumer trust in online reviews. The
     Pew data was fielded in late 2015, before the FTC's 2023 review
     manipulation rulemaking. Direction of any shift is unknown.
 18. Section numbering in the adopted 2025 state water ordinance. The DWR PDF
     is a rulemaking redline carrying prior and adopted text, so
     applicability appears under both an old and a new section number. Any
     section cited to a client or agency should be confirmed against the
     codified 23 CCR text.
 19. Adoption and effective dates of the water ordinance text used here. The
     PDF carries no adoption date on its face and interleaves 2015 and
     newly numbered sections, so which text governs a given job date is
     not resolvable from that file.
 20. The new section 494 Certificate of Completion Package under the
     prescriptive and performance compliance options. Referenced in the
     adopted text but its body is absent from the fetched document.
     Confirm against the codified regulation before finalising a closeout
     packet.
 21. Operative water ordinance thresholds in Redwood City, San Mateo County and
     neighbouring cities. Local agencies may adopt stricter ordinances, so
     the 500 and 2,500 square foot figures are a floor. Requires reading
     each local ordinance and its forms.
 22. USDA hardiness zone for the actual towns served, which the caliper based
     tree establishment rule depends on. Confirm against the current USDA
     map before adopting a caliper based warranty term.
 23. Local Peninsula minimum wage ordinances were not checked. Several San
     Mateo County cities exceed the state rate. Requires checking each
     city's ordinance directly.
 24. Currency of several statutory texts. Pages for CCP 337.1, CCP 337.15, B&P
     7091 and Civil Code 896 were fetched without capturing an enactment
     or amendment date, so those findings cannot show their own age.
 25. The eCFR source notes for 16 CFR Parts 239 and 255 could not be read
     because the URL redirects to an interstitial. Part 255's 2023
     revision date came from the FTC press release. Part 239 is cited
     undated.
 26. Whether 16 CFR Part 239, the warranty advertising guides, applies to
     construction service warranties rather than consumer product
     warranties. Its own examples concern returning goods in original
     packaging. A California construction attorney is the right source.
 27. Typical workmanship warranty terms offered by landscape contractors: no
     survey at any acceptable tier reports what share offer a written
     warranty or for how long. Everything located was anecdotal trade
     copy.
 28. Plant establishment and replacement guarantee norms. The one year is
     standard claim cannot be traced to any source disclosing a sample or
     method. The one year figure surviving here rests on regulation, not
     on evidence of practice.
 29. Callback and rework rates specific to landscape design build: nothing at
     any tier. All rework research covers vertical building construction.
     The firm's own callback history is the only available source.
 30. Callback and rework economics for firms under about ten employees: nothing
     found. The rework literature samples large contractors and production
     builders.
 31. Rework cost as a share of contract value. The 0.38 and 0.76 percent
     figures traced to a society news article with no disclosed sample and
     were dropped. The underlying peer reviewed study exists and should be
     obtained through a library.
 32. Secondhand rework ranges of 2 to 6 percent and 3.15 percent appear in a
     literature review that was read directly, but neither original paper
     could be fetched. Recorded as unverified secondhand citations only.
 33. The often quoted claim that rework averages 5 percent of total project
     cost was not confirmed against any primary publication and is not
     cited.
 34. The relationship between documented handover and dispute frequency: no
     empirical study at any tier. The nearest proxy is the licensing
     board's insufficient evidence closure rate, which is suggestive
     rather than a study of the question.
 35. Licensing board complaint data broken out by licence classification. The
     enforcement report does not disaggregate, so there is no California
     figure for complaints against C-27 landscaping contractors
     specifically.
 36. Willingness to pay for a builder provided maintenance program: the full
     text returned HTTP 403, so the roughly $27 a month figure was struck.
     Requires library access to the article.
 37. Whether offering a written warranty changes which contractor a homeowner
     hires. The only tiered study found measures stated willingness to pay
     for new home warranties, not selection.
 38. Residential maintenance churn or retention rate: nothing at T1 or T2. The
     only candidates are commercial weighted trade press that could not be
     fetched. The firm's own account history over three years would answer
     it.
 39. The break even between a price increase and the risk of losing a recurring
     account: no published empirical answer in any field service industry.
     Derivable from the firm's own gross margin per account and
     replacement acquisition cost, which is more trustworthy anyway.
 40. Distribution of actual annual increases applied by landscape maintenance
     firms, and the retention effect of a given increase size. Unmeasured.
     The marketing literature establishes direction only and its sample
     sizes remain behind paywalls.
 41. Drive time as a share of the landscape maintenance workday: no measured
     figure at any tier. The circulating wrench time benchmarks trace to
     field service software marketing. One month of truck GPS or
     timesheets would produce a better number than anything published.
 42. Cost per stop as a function of route density in a real residential grounds
     maintenance operation. The routing literature is analytical or
     simulated and has never been calibrated on this trade.
 43. Route density effect on the crew day rather than on driving. Every source
     addresses travel distance or time; none addresses the share of a crew
     day that is drive time, which is the ratio that decides whether
     density yields another billable stop.
 44. No producer price index exists for landscaping services. The nearest
     output price measure, the gardening and lawncare consumer price item,
     is thinly published with no annual average after 2023, so the 4.8
     percent escalation benchmark cannot be extended from a government
     source.
 45. No Bay Area price index exists for landscape maintenance services
     specifically. Comparing the national gardening item against the local
     all items index mixes geographies and periods and is indicative only.
 46. No survey at any tier isolates firms under twenty employees or under $1M
     revenue. The largest software surveys publish no size breakdown and
     sample commercial contractors; the one disclosed association sample
     of 142 firms is spread thin across bands and regions.
 47. No independent, non vendor measurement of outcomes from business
     management platform adoption exists. No controlled study, no before
     and after design, no third party replication. Only a vendor comparing
     its own customers with non customers, uncontrolled for size.
 48. No source at any tier reports failure or abandonment rates for business
     management software among small contractors, nor any switching cost
     figure: no implementation hours, migration cost, productivity loss or
     cost of a failed adoption. The only hard switching cost documented is
     one vendor's auto renewing annual contract with 60 day notice.
 49. Total cost of ownership cannot be established for any vendor from public
     information. One onboarding fee is referenced but never quantified,
     another vendor's per user and implementation rates are unpublished
     and its tier prices contradict each other on its own page, a third
     publishes nothing, and every top tier is quote only.
 50. One vendor's Grow tier price could not be pinned down: two direct fetches
     of the same page returned incompatible numbers. Must be confirmed
     directly from the vendor before any cost comparison.
 51. One vendor's supposed $1M revenue entry point is not on its own page,
     which names no minimum, and traces only to a competitor's comparison
     page. Its real minimum viable customer size is undocumented.
 52. No source addresses whether any platform models selling design as a
     separate paid step priced as a share of construction. This is
     arguably the most decision relevant software question and can only be
     answered by direct product evaluation.
 53. No source directly measures whether firms keep a separate estimating tool
     alongside a platform. The tool count figures are the nearest proxy
     and do not name which categories are duplicated or separate platform
     users from non users.
 54. The most quoted job costing statistic in the trade, 46 percent using job
     costing software alongside a 60 percent estimate accuracy figure,
     traces to a commissioned survey that does not disclose the landscape
     subset's sample or selection method. Not cited.
 55. A digital adoption survey reporting that about half name training and
     implementation as the biggest barrier was rejected: it discloses a
     respondent count but nothing about selection, screening or field
     dates, and draws on the vendor's own customer base. The underlying
     idea, that implementation capacity rather than price decides success
     for a small firm, remains plausible and unevidenced.
 56. Two trade publications' industry reports remain unfetchable, returning
     empty bodies on repeated attempts. Their circulating figures on
     technology reliance, retention, net margin and difficulty raising
     prices exist only in search extractions. Someone should open those
     pages in a real browser and read the methodology box.
 57. One trade publication's service mix breakdown, showing design build
     offered by 62 percent of surveyed companies, discloses no sample size
     or recruitment method anywhere and fails even the lowest tier.
 58. The remodelers' cost of doing business study segments design build against
     general remodelers, which would be the closest analogue to whether
     selling design separately pays, but no sample size is disclosed
     publicly and the segmentation is behind a paid publication.
 59. The association's financial benchmark report is the one industry benchmark
     that discloses its sample and segments gross profit by design build
     versus maintenance, but those figures sit behind the paywall. If it
     is bought, read the respondent profile appendix first.
 60. A consumer survey on homeowner use of lawn and landscape services could
     not be fetched from the primary source, which returned HTTP 403.
     Trade press discloses enough method to qualify it, but it is nine
     years old and measures service usage rather than published practice.
 61. The advocacy summary attaching specific margins to the peer reviewed
     delivery study carries no publication date and its headline faster
     delivery figure is undefined on the page. It should not be quoted
     without the underlying foundation report.
 62. Sample sizes for both cited marketing papers remain unknown. Publisher
     sites returned HTTP 403 or CAPTCHA. Only abstracts were reachable, so
     both support direction only, never a magnitude.
 63. A cited restaurant rating study appeared only in search snippets;
     publisher pages returned HTTP 403 or no article content. Deliberately
     not cited.
 64. The government bulk wage files are not retrievable from this environment,
     returning bot blocks on repeated attempts. All current wage figures
     were re sourced through the public data API. Anyone re verifying
     should use the API or a browser rather than command line downloads.
 65. The entire labour cost section was anchored to a superseded wage release
     in earlier drafts. The current release moved the local premium over
     national from about 27 percent to about 23 percent and the crew lead
     premium from about 49 to about 60 percent. Any downstream calculation
     built on the older release needs re running.
 66. No verified prior year California or national wage baseline exists here,
     because the state and national bulk files are blocked. The earlier
     California median figure could not be traced and was removed.
 67. Structure of residential maintenance agreements: no T1 or T2 evidence on
     typical term length, cancellation notice or renewal mechanism. What
     exists is public agency commercial contracts, which are one sided by
     design, plus California statutory constraints. The gap between them
     is where this firm has to make its own policy choice.
 68. Whether the automatic renewal law applies to a bespoke, in person signed
     residential service agreement with a licensed contractor is not
     settled by the bill text, and it was not checked for later
     amendments. Counsel should confirm before any auto renewing
     maintenance agreement is issued.
 69. Licensing board consumer pages carry no publication or last updated date
     and cite no code sections, so their currency cannot be established
     from the page itself. The underlying statutes should be read directly
     before any guidance is relied on.
 70. One board publication remains unreachable at its short URL, returning HTTP
     404. The equivalent guidance is now sourced from the printed
     brochure, so nothing of substance is lost.
 71. No T1 source exists for the software adoption question at all. Searches
     for peer reviewed or extension work on business management software
     among landscape contractors returned nothing landscape specific.
     Every adoption, pricing and outcome figure here is T2 or T3, and all
     but one T3 source is published by a company selling the software
     being measured.
 72. No California or Peninsula specific data on contractor software adoption,
     pricing or practice exists at any tier. The census firm size data is
     the only California specific evidence in that section, and it says
     nothing about software.
 73. Nothing in this base is specific to the San Francisco Peninsula, to four
     person firms, or to California landscape contractors as a class,
     outside the regulatory findings. Every quantitative finding is
     national, from another sector, or both, and none of the local ones is
     about sales process.
 74. A note on tooling, recorded because an earlier audit reached conclusions
     by invoking it: the claim that PDF text extraction was broken in this
     environment is false. Extraction works, and the strongest finding in
     the set was recovered that way. Any earlier conclusion excused by
     broken PDF tooling should be re checked.
 75. A gap closed and recorded so it is not reopened: the self diagnosis field
     experiment was previously listed as blocked by a paywall. A free full
     text working paper version exists, giving country, sample size and
     absolute effect. The paywall blocked only the journal version, not
     the evidence.
