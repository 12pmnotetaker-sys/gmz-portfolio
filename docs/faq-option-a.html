Getting started

Before you have spoken to anyone.

Does the site visit cost anything?

No. The first site visit is free. Someone comes out, walks the property with you, looks at access, grade, drainage and what is already growing there, and talks through what you want. Booking it commits you to nothing.

Two things are worth knowing before it happens. The visit is a working conversation rather than a sales call, and it is also where GMZ works out whether this is a project we are the right contractor for, so expect direct questions about scope, timing and what you are planning to spend. And a visit is not a bid. What can come out of it is a shared understanding of the work and, when it makes sense, a complimentary concept and a planning range to think about.

How soon can someone come out and look at my yard?

It depends on the week, and you will get an actual date rather than a promise that someone will call you back. Tell us your town and what you want done, and you will hear what is genuinely open. Landscape work here runs on the weather, so spring books up faster than late fall.

If your project has a date attached to it, say so on that first call: an event, a house going on the market, a permit clock, a school year, a family visit. It changes what can honestly be promised, and it is far better known at the start than discovered halfway through.

Which towns do you work in?

GMZ works the San Francisco Peninsula and is based in Redwood City. The towns we work in regularly are Redwood City, Atherton, Menlo Park, Palo Alto, Woodside, San Carlos, Belmont, Portola Valley, Los Altos and Hillsborough.

If you are near that list but not on it, ask. For a build project a little extra distance is usually workable, because the crew parks once in the morning and works the same ground all day. For maintenance it matters more: maintenance runs on fixed-day routes, so a property well away from an existing route may not be one we can serve properly. We would rather tell you that than take the account and become the contractor who keeps arriving late.

What size projects do you take? Is my job too small?

There is no published minimum job size, so ask about your job rather than talk yourself out of calling. GMZ does full residential design and build work: planting, irrigation, drainage, patios, walls, fences and lighting. It also runs a maintenance book of weekly and biweekly accounts, so work that is too small to mobilize a build crew for is often better handled as a maintenance visit or a short service call.

What decides it is not square footage. It is whether the work justifies bringing a crew, a truck and equipment to your property at all. An afternoon of pruning and a new patio are different kinds of job, not different sizes of the same job, and they are priced and contracted differently.

Say what you want done on the first call and you will get a straight answer, including "that is not the right fit for us" if that is the answer. A contractor who takes a job that is wrong for them serves it badly, and you feel it in the schedule.

What actually happens when I call?

A short conversation, then a decision about whether a site visit makes sense for both of us. Expect to be asked what you want done, where the property is, when you would like it done, who else is part of the decision, and what you are planning to spend. That last question is the one people brace for, and it is asked as much for your benefit as ours: it is how you find out early whether GMZ is in the right range for your project, rather than after a design is already underway.

You will not get a price on that call. Landscape work is priced off a measured site and chosen materials, and anyone pricing your yard over the phone is guessing at both. What you can get on the call is whether this is work GMZ does, whether your property is in the area we serve, and a date to walk it.

Do I need to have drawings or a plan before I call you?

No. Most people call with photos and a rough idea, and that is enough to start. If you already have drawings from an architect or a landscape designer, GMZ can build to them; bring them to the visit and you will get an honest read on whether they are buildable as drawn and what is missing. If you have nothing, GMZ designs as well as builds, and the design step is its own paid stage under its own signed agreement.

What is needed before a firm price is a measured site and decided materials, and that is exactly what the design step produces. It is also why a number given before that point is a planning range rather than a bid. Nobody can price a yard accurately from photographs, and a contractor who says otherwise is guessing with your money.

What should I send you before you come out?

Photos and a few plain facts, sent ahead, make the visit much more useful. Send photos of the whole area from several angles, including the parts you are unhappy with, and anything you have already collected: a plot plan or survey, drawings from an architect or designer, HOA rules that apply to your yard, and pictures of yards you like.

Two details are worth writing down because they change the work more than people expect. First, how a truck and a small machine would reach the work area, and how wide the narrowest gate is. Second, whether water collects anywhere after rain. Also useful: what you want kept, and what you have already decided versus what is still open.

For a maintenance account it is simpler. Photos of the property, whether there is an irrigation system and roughly how it is set up, and which day of the week suits you. None of this is required. If you would rather just talk, call and bring the rest to the visit.

Your concept and planning budget

The first numbers, and why they are a range.

Why won't you just give me a price?

Because nobody has measured your property yet, and a single number before that would be a guess dressed up as a commitment. The first number you get from GMZ is a range. It is built from a walk of the site, photos and rough dimensions, not from a measured survey and a final materials list. Those two things move a landscape price more than anything else, and neither is settled at this point.

Here is how much they move it. The surface material you choose can move the biggest part of the price a long way, and the same patio in a different material is a different project financially. What sits under the existing surface, and whether a truck can reach the back of the property, changes the labor before a single thing is installed. We can tell you the honest window your project sits in today. We cannot hand you a fixed price for work that has not been drawn or measured, and a number given before that work is done usually turns into a change order later.

The number tightens as the site is measured and the materials are chosen. Measuring costs you nothing.

Can you just give me a ballpark over the phone?

Not a number, no. A price given by someone who has not seen your property is either useless or misleading, and it tends to become the figure you remember for the rest of the project. We would rather not start you there.

What a phone call is good for is deciding quickly whether we are a fit. If what you are describing and what you want to spend are not in the same neighborhood, we will say so on the call rather than after the drive. If they are, we book a visit. The visit is free, and it is where the useful conversation actually happens.

If you are asking about maintenance rather than a build, the answer is the same for the same reason: we walk the property first. Maintenance is priced separately from construction work and runs under its own service agreement, with its own schedule and its own terms.

How wide is the range going to be, and why so wide?

It is wide, and we would rather be plain about that than pretend otherwise. At the concept stage the top of the range sits well above the bottom, because the two things that decide the final number have not happened yet: nobody has measured the site, and you have not chosen the materials. Both of those are real work, and doing them properly is what closes the gap.

The range narrows in steps. It narrows when we measure. It narrows when you pick a surface material and a plant palette. It narrows when we know whether the existing surface comes out and where the debris goes, and again when current prices come back on the specific items in your plan. Once there is a measured plan and selected materials, the number is tight enough to commit to, and that is the point at which we put a fixed price in front of you.

We will also tell you which parts of your range are the loose ones. Usually it is demolition, drainage and anything underground, because what is beneath the surface is unknown until somebody digs.

Is the planning budget a quote?

No. It is a planning number, not a bid and not a fixed price, and the document says exactly that on its face. GMZ calls it a Preliminary Budget and Scope Acknowledgment. Its job is to tell you what to plan around, and to put the scope in writing so we are both looking at the same project.

What it is: a range for the work described, broken into the areas of your property so you can see where the money sits. What it is not: an offer we are bound to, a price we can hold, or a promise about work nobody has measured. A number you can sign comes later, after a measured plan and chosen materials.

We work this way because a planning budget is the only honest thing to hand someone who needs to know what to expect before they have bought a design. The alternative is a firm-sounding number written over an unmeasured yard, and the person holding the difference in that arrangement is you.

If I accept the planning budget, what have I committed to?

Nothing. Acknowledging the planning budget is not a contract to build and it does not obligate you to spend anything. You are confirming three things: that you have seen the scope we described, that you understand the number is a planning range rather than a fixed price, and that you would like to keep going.

It exists to prevent one specific problem, which is two people remembering the scope differently three months later. Everything we priced is listed. Everything we did not price is listed as well, and that second list is the one worth reading slowly, because it is where most disputes in this trade actually live.

The next commitment is a separate document and it is a real one: the design agreement, which is signed and paid. The construction contract is separate again, and comes after that. Nothing before those two obligates you to build anything.

Is the concept really free, or is there a catch?

It is complimentary today, and there is no catch attached to it. Before you buy any design work, GMZ will walk your property, put together a concept for what could happen there, and give you a planning budget to go with it. You are not billed for that and you are not obliged to go further.

What is not free is the design itself. The measured plans, the material selections and the drawings a crew can actually build from are paid work, under their own signed design agreement. That step is priced as a percentage of construction, in a band of 10 to 15 percent. The design fee is not credited back against the construction price, and we say so plainly because most people assume the opposite. It pays for design time, which is real time by real people, and it has value whether or not GMZ ends up building the project.

The concept exists so you can decide whether this project is worth designing at all. If the answer is no, you have spent nothing.

What if the budget comes back higher than I was expecting?

Say so, and tell us the number you had in mind. That is a normal conversation and it is far better to have it now than after a design has been drawn to a scope you cannot fund. A planning budget that lands above your expectation is doing its job: it found the gap while closing it is still cheap.

There are three usual ways to close it. The first is scope: take something out or make it smaller. A smaller paved area, fewer built structures, planting instead of a hard surface. The second is material choice, which is the biggest single lever on most projects, because the same design in a different surface material is a different price. The third is phasing the work over time, which we cover separately.

What we will not do is quietly thin the work to hit a number. Cutting base depth, drainage or soil preparation makes a job cheaper on paper and more expensive in a few years, and you are the one who pays that second bill. If a scope genuinely cannot be built properly for your budget, we would rather tell you than take the job.

Can we do the project in phases to spread the cost?

Yes, and it is common. Phasing is a real option rather than a consolation prize. It works best when every phase is drawn on one plan at the start, so that the property is not built twice.

The order matters more than the split does. Grading, drainage and anything that has to run under a patio or a walkway goes first, even when the visible part of that area is years away. Sleeves for irrigation and lighting are inexpensive while the ground is open and costly once there is concrete over them. Planting often makes a good later phase, since plants fill in over time regardless of when they go in.

Two things to know before you plan around it. Each phase is its own agreement with its own price, and that price is set when the phase is priced rather than years in advance. Phasing also costs somewhat more in total than doing the same work at once, because the setup and cleanup happen more than once. It is still the right answer for plenty of projects.

The design

What you are buying, and what it does for the price.

Why do I have to pay for a design? Can't you just draw something up?

Because the design is real work, and it is the part that makes everything after it accurate. Before you pay anything, you get a complimentary concept and a written planning budget: a range to plan around, not a bid. That is enough to decide whether the project is worth going further with. It is not enough to build from.

The paid design step is what turns that concept into something buildable. Your property is measured in detail and drawn to scale. Levels, drainage and access get worked out. Plants and materials get chosen and drawn in place. Those hours are the same hours whether we end up building the project or not, so they are sold as their own step rather than folded into a construction price you have not agreed to yet.

Design is covered by its own signed agreement, separate from any construction contract. You see the scope and the fee in writing before you commit to it.

How much do you charge for design?

Design is priced as a percentage of the construction price, and the band is 10 to 15 percent. That is a common way landscape design is priced, and it is the same band printed on the planning budget you receive before any of this is decided, so you can work out roughly what design will run as soon as you have a construction range.

Where a project lands inside that band depends on how much design there is to do: how many separate areas are in scope, how much grading and drainage has to be resolved, how much irrigation, and whether anything needs outside review. A single planting bed and a whole-property renovation are not the same amount of drawing.

We do not put a design fee in front of you before there is a construction range to apply it to, which is why the complimentary concept and the planning budget come first. The fee, and exactly what it covers, go into the design agreement before you sign it.

If I hire you to build it, does the design fee come off the construction price?

No. The design fee is not credited toward construction, and it is not refunded when you build with us. We say that plainly and early because plenty of clients expect it to be credited, and contract signing is the worst possible moment to find out otherwise.

The reason is straightforward. Design and construction are two separate pieces of work, and each carries its own price. The design hours are spent before a shovel moves, and they are spent whether or not you go ahead. If the fee were credited, the construction price would simply have to carry those hours somewhere you could not see them. We would rather show you both numbers and let you judge each one on its own.

What the fee buys is a plan drawn from real measurements of your property, and a construction price built from that plan instead of from a guess.

What do I actually get for the design fee, and do I own the drawings?

You get a measured plan set for your property: a site plan drawn to scale from real measurements, the layout of paving, walls, steps and beds, a planting plan showing what goes where, irrigation and drainage resolved, and materials specified closely enough to price and build from. You also get the firm construction number that comes out of those plans, which is something a concept sketch cannot produce.

On ownership, the position we recommend and expect to put in writing is this: you receive the drawings and are free to keep them and use them for your property, and GMZ retains the copyright in the drawings themselves. That language is being finalized now.

So ask to see the ownership clause in the design agreement before you sign, and if it is not in there yet, ask for it. A design you paid for should come with a written answer about what you can do with it.

How long does the design take?

For a straightforward residential project, weeks rather than months. That is our recommended position and we are in the middle of committing it to writing, so treat it as an honest expectation rather than a published term.

The clock starts when your property is measured, not when you sign. What drives it after that is mostly on the decision side: how many areas are in scope, how quickly plant and material selections get settled, and whether anything needs outside review. A design where the choices land quickly moves quickly, and one that waits on a decision waits.

Ask for a target presentation date to be written into the design agreement. Any date we give you should be one you can hold us to.

What if I don't like the first design you show me?

Adding a whole new area of the property, or changing what the project is, is new scope, and it moves both the design and the construction number.

I already have plans from a landscape architect. Will you build from them?

Your firm price

The number you can commit to, and what sits behind it.

What's actually included in the price, and what isn't?

Both are listed in writing in the bid, before you sign anything. Included is every item of work we have priced: the materials and their level, the labor, the equipment, the haul-off and the daily site services. Excluded is named just as plainly, item by item. On a residential landscape job the usual exclusions are permit fees, arborist reports, work inside the house, utility upgrades, rock or buried concrete that could not be seen from the surface, and anything the plan does not show.

A named exclusion is more honest than a padded number. If we quietly added money to cover things that might not happen, you would pay for them whether or not they happened, and you would have no way to tell how much of your price was real work and how much was insurance against a maybe. Naming an exclusion tells you exactly where your price ends and lets you decide what to do about it: bring the item in now, price it separately, or accept the risk knowingly.

Read the exclusions page first. It is the shortest page in the document and it is where nearly every contractor dispute actually lives. If something you assumed was included is sitting on that page, ask us before you sign and we will price it.

Why is the bid different from the budget range you gave me earlier?

Because the earlier number was a planning range built from a walk-through and a concept, and this one is built from a measured site and materials you have chosen. That is the entire difference, and it is the reason this number can be committed to and the first one could not.

Three things move it, and we name which ones moved yours. The site gets measured, so quantities stop being read off photographs. Materials get chosen, and on hardscape the gap between a basic paver and natural stone over the same area is a multiple, not a percentage. And the scope settles: demolition and haul-off come in or stay out, and drainage gets assessed rather than assumed. Any of those three can move a number in either direction, and the measured price sometimes comes in under the planning range.

If the bid lands outside the range you were given, we tell you before you open the document and we name the reason. Every real gap has one. A number that moved for no reason anybody can name means we got something wrong, and we would rather find that ourselves than have you find it.

How long is this price good for?

Until the date printed on the face of the bid. We normally set that at thirty days from the day we issue it, and after that we re-check material prices before we honor the number.

Thirty days is not an arbitrary deadline. Material prices in this trade move on their own schedule: stone, lumber, concrete and plant stock especially. The prices sitting behind your number carry expiry dates of their own. A month is long enough for you to think it over and short enough that we are not committing to a price nobody can still buy.

If you go past the date, tell us rather than assuming the number is dead. Most of the time it barely moves and we simply re-issue it. Where something has genuinely changed, we show you which line changed and why, instead of handing you a new total and letting you guess.

We do this because the material level is the largest single lever in a landscape price, and there is no honest midpoint to guess at. Pricing one tier and calling it the answer hides the decision from you. Seeing the same patio priced two ways puts the trade in front of you in dollars, which is where it belongs. We will also tell you where we think the money works hardest: a smaller area in the better material usually reads better than a large area in the cheaper one, and there are places where nobody will ever notice the difference.

The same applies to phasing. If the whole scope is more than you want to commit to now, we can price it in stages, so the parts that only happen once go in together: base, drainage and irrigation lines. Who pulls the permits, and who pays for them?

GMZ files the permit and manages the inspections as the licensed contractor of record, and the fee itself is billed to you as a separate line at whatever the town charges, not folded into the price.

Most residential landscape work on the Peninsula needs no permit at all. The work that commonly does: a retaining wall over a certain height, structures you can stand under, electrical, gas lines, anything that changes how water leaves your property, and on larger planting and irrigation projects, the state's landscape water-efficiency review. Every town on the Peninsula sets its own thresholds and its own timeline, so we tell you at bid time which of these we expect to apply to your job and roughly what it does to the start date.

The fee is excluded from the price rather than estimated into it for a simple reason: the town sets it, it varies by jurisdiction and by valuation, and we cannot know it before the application goes in. Naming a guess and then correcting it later is worse than naming it as a pass-through from the start.

Building it

What the weeks on site actually look like.

How far out are you booked?

It changes month to month, so ask us and we will give you the real date rather than a comfortable one. We check the work already committed against the calendar before we promise a start, which is why we do not post a booking window on the website: a number published there is wrong by the time most people read it.

What does not change is how the queue works. A start date is held by a signed contract, not by a conversation, and until the paperwork is done your slot is not yours. We will say that plainly rather than let you assume otherwise and then find out in week six. Materials with a long lead are ordered as soon as the contract is in: specimen trees, stone from a particular quarry, custom metal. That ordering window, rather than crew availability, is often what sets the earliest honest start.

If you have a hard date, an event, a house sale, a school year, a permit clock, tell us in the first conversation. It changes what we recommend, it sometimes changes the sequence of the build, and occasionally it is the reason we tell you we are not the right contractor for this one.

How long is my yard going to be a construction site?

Longer than the number of working days on the schedule, and you should hear that from us now rather than discover it in week three. A build is not continuous work. There are days of machines and noise, then a quiet stretch waiting on a concrete cure, an inspection or a delivery, then noise again. Your yard is out of use from the first day of demolition to the last day of cleanup, quiet stretches included.

The shape of it is fairly consistent. The roughest part is at the front: demolition, grading and drainage, which is where the noise, the dust and the machine traffic all live. Expect part of your driveway or street frontage to be staging for material and debris, and we will tell you which part before we start. If plants are staying, irrigation may be off for a stretch and we will tell you what needs hand watering and when. The back half of a job is quieter and finer: planting, irrigation adjustment, lighting and cleanup.

You get a working schedule at the pre-construction meeting and an update once a week on the same day: what changed, what is next, and anything that needs a decision from you. Three things move a landscape schedule: weather, inspections, and what we find underground. Each has its own answer in this list, and none of them is a surprise we spring on you at the end.

How much do you need up front, and when do I pay the rest?

Less than most people expect, because California limits it. On a home improvement contract the down payment cannot exceed the lesser of $1,000 or 10 percent of the contract price. That is state law and it exists to protect you. Any residential contractor asking for a third up front is not following it, and that is worth knowing whoever you hire.

For the rest, payments fall due against milestones as work is completed, never on a calendar. A payment is triggered by something finished that you can walk out and look at, and each milestone is named in the contract before you sign, so there is no interpretation left for later. The final payment comes after the walkthrough and after the punch list is cleared, not before.

One thing that catches people out: the design step runs under its own separate signed agreement, with its own terms, and it is paid separately from construction. The design fee is not a deposit on the build and it is not credited toward it. That is the single most common assumption clients bring to this conversation, so we answer it early rather than at signing.

What's a change order, and how do I avoid getting hit with a pile of them?

A change order is a written amendment to the contract, priced and signed by both of us before the work happens, covering anything that was not in the original scope. There are three causes and two of them are avoidable. Scope you add mid-job, which is fine and is what change orders are for: you see the wall going up and want it longer. Decisions made late, a surface chosen after the base is already in. And genuine unknowns underground, which is the third and is the reason the exclusions page exists at all.

What happens when it rains?

Work that rain would ruin stops; work it would not carries on. Compaction, concrete, paving, grading and planting into saturated soil all fail if you force them, so those move. Fence posts, irrigation repair, pruning, cleanup and most maintenance visits go ahead in the wet.

A weather delay is not a change order and does not add anything to your price. It moves the schedule, and we tell you the same week what moved and what the new sequence looks like. What we will not do is push a weather-sensitive task through because a date was promised. A patio set on soft base looks fine for one season and then tells on you for years afterward, and that is our problem as much as yours.

From late autumn through early spring we build float into the schedule for this rather than pretending the season is dry, and we leave the site safe ahead of a storm: exposed soil covered, open trenches protected, and water still leaving your property the way it is supposed to.

What happens if you dig and find something you weren't expecting?

We stop, photograph it, show it to you, price the fix and get your signature before we carry on. You will not learn about it from the final invoice.

What actually turns up in Peninsula yards: an old slab or footing under the lawn, rock, an abandoned irrigation line, a septic remnant, tree roots at a size where an arborist ought to look at them, a drain that goes nowhere, and utility lines that are not where any map says. None of it is visible from the surface, which is precisely why it sits on the exclusions page instead of inside your price. Do you call 811 before digging, or is that something I have to arrange?

Yes, every job, and no, you do not arrange it. GMZ places the utility locate request before any digging on your property. It is free, it is required in California, and the marks have to be on the ground before a shovel goes in. The request carries a required notice period, so it goes in ahead of the first day of work rather than being called in that morning.

One limit is worth knowing. The locate service marks public utilities in the work area by color: gas, electric, water, communications. What does a brand new garden actually need in its first year?

Water, delivered the right way, matters more than everything else combined in the first year. New plants are growing roots, not showing off. Water deeply and less often so the moisture goes down and the roots follow it; short daily sprinkles keep roots at the surface and make a plant that cannot survive its first hot week. If something looks stressed between cycles, hand water that plant rather than raising the schedule for the whole garden.

Two other things carry most of the rest. Keep mulch topped up and pulled back off the stems, and pull weeds while they are small, because freshly worked soil grows them fast. Do not push fertilizer in the first season and do not prune new shrubs hard: both work against a plant that is trying to root. Expect some settling in new beds and paths, and expect a few plants to sit and sulk for a season before they move.

By the second summer the garden can run on a normal schedule. The first year is the one that decides whether it gets there, which is also why we always offer to maintain it ourselves.

What do you actually do on a maintenance visit?

How do you price maintenance?

On how much your property takes to service properly, and how often we come. We walk the property before pricing anything: how much lawn there is, how much planted bed, how much pruning it will want across a year, how much green waste comes off it, and how easily equipment moves around it.

Should I be on a weekly or an every-other-week schedule?

Weekly if you have lawn, a lot of planted bed, or you want the property to look tidy every day of the week. Every other week works well for gardens that are mostly established shrubs, groundcover, gravel or decomposed granite, where growth is slow and there is not much to pick up.

The honest test is the growing season, not the winter. From spring into early summer, a lawn on a two-week cycle can look shaggy well before the next visit, and the beds put on enough growth that the visit stops being maintenance and starts being catch-up. Plenty of properties want weekly through the growing months and settle into every other week once things slow down.
